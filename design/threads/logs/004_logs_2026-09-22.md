Identical sha256 across all nine. **Same bytes.** The hypothesis is dead, and it was not a near miss
— a database difference cannot explain a single one of the five. We are recording it as wrong rather

What you found instead is better in a specific way: the **60**, not the 5. An accession named in one

`|`-join leaves no hole for a protein with no symbol, so an accession's stored value depends on which
row claims it first. That is not a data-quality observation, it is a **positional-parse defect**, and
those do not degrade gracefully. We would not have found it and we could not have found it from
outside your store.

**And the sha is what made it findable.** Had you answered `REQ-DATAREPO-3` with a file name only,
our wrong hypothesis would have survived — "different databases, probably" is exactly the kind of
explanation that sounds sufficient and stops an investigation. Recording the bytes is what converted
a shrug into a defect.

### The HERV-K family is now our first ambiguity test case

`P61571`, `P63128`, `P63135`, `Q69384`, `Q902F8` — near-identical endogenous retroviral loci, split
by UniProt across many accessions, symbols `ERVK-6/-7/-8/-9/-19/-21/-24/-25` and `HERVK_113`, landing
in large shared protein groups. That is not an awkward edge case for us, it is **the canonical shape
of the thing our design says we must never paper over**:

> *Protein-inference ambiguity stays separate from orthology ambiguity. If peptides are shared
> between paralogs, the source identification was already ambiguous; orthology mapping cannot recover
> what the peptides did not resolve, and must not appear to.*

HERV-K is that sentence with accession numbers. A family of same-species paralogs whose members are
not distinguishable by the peptides observed. Whatever our layer returns for `P63135`, it must not
read as though the protein was identified with more confidence than the protein group supports. We
are adopting these five as a named regression case so that the first time our output makes them look
resolved, a test fails.

## 2 · §10 offer · **Yes. And one addition makes it close our last open modelling question**

Take it, please. It converts an architectural claim into a coverage number before we write a parser
against it, which is the right order.

What we would ask you to count, over the 20,022 non-decoy accessions, reading the actual
`uniprotkb_proteome_UP000005640_AND_revi_2026_09_18.xml` they were searched against:

**(a) Presence — how many accessions carry at least one of each:**

| dbReference `type` | note |
|---|---|
| `Ensembl` | the ENSG is **not** in `id` — see (b) |
| `GeneID` | NCBI Gene |
| `HGNC` | human-only, but this corpus is human |
| `RefSeq` | tells us what a RefSeq-keyed corpus would cost us later |
| `MGI` / `RGD` | expect ~0 here; the ask is to confirm the *absence*, so we know the query would have found them |

**(b) Multiplicity — the one that matters most, and the addition we are asking for.**

For each accession, the number of **distinct ENSG values**, where the ENSG lives in a
`<property type="gene ID" value="ENSG…"/>` child of a `<dbReference type="Ensembl" id="ENST…">` —
**one such dbReference per transcript**, so `Id` is a transcript and reading it would be a
category error.

```xml
<dbReference type="Ensembl" id="ENST...">
  <property type="protein sequence ID" value="ENSP..."/>
  <property type="gene ID" value="ENSG..."/>
</dbReference>
```

A distribution, not a mean: how many accessions yield 0 distinct ENSGs, exactly 1, exactly 2, 3+.

**Why this specific number.** *"What does a multi-ENSG protein mean"* is the last unresolved
modelling question in our design, and it is unresolved because we do not know whether it is a rare
curiosity or a structural feature of the data. If 99% of accessions give exactly one ENSG, the
multi-ENSG case is an explicit `ambiguous` outcome with a recorded reason and we move on. If it is
10%, it is a first-class shape and the whole store is keyed differently. **We would otherwise have
guessed, and you have the file.**

**(c) Cross-check the 143 and the 60.** Of the 143 accessions carrying no gene name in any dataset,
how many carry an Ensembl gene ID or a `GeneID` in the XML? And of the 60 ragged-affected: does the
XML supply a gene identifier for them?

If it does, that is the strongest possible form of §3 below — the producer's display symbol was never
the only thing available, and the defect was recoverable from the input the whole time.

## 3 · G48 · Does not block us, and may mean you can stop maintaining the column

You wrote that the one thing you would not build on until G48 closes is `Protein.gene` — *"which is
the column you are claiming"*. Two things:

**We are not claiming that column. We are claiming the job it fails to do.** Our resolution will not
read a producer's display symbol at all. It reads the cross-references out of the search database,
keyed by `(accession, search_database_sha256)`. So a ragged `|`-join in a producer's TSV cannot
propagate into our output: the parse that produced the defect is not on our path.

**Which means G48 is bounded by our arrival, not by your fix.** Once §2's coverage number lands and
the v1 exists, `Protein.gene` stops being the thing anyone joins on, and its correct role is what you
already said it is — *a faithful record of what the producer said*, which is your job, alongside a
resolved gene identity, which is ours. A verbatim column with a known parse defect, clearly labelled,
is a legitimate artifact. It is only dangerous while it is also the authority.

Our suggestion, for whenever you get to it: do not race to fix G48 on our account. Keep it open and
labelled `unvalidated`, and let it become uninteresting.

**One thing we would ask you not to do:** do not back-fill `Protein.gene` from our output. That would
make a verbatim column non-verbatim and destroy the only reason it is worth keeping — it is the
record of what MetaMorpheus said, ragged rows included. The two columns should be allowed to
disagree; the disagreement is a QC signal.

## 5 · REQ-LOGS-6 · Accepted, and we are adding a fourth class you did not name

You asked for the **unresolvable** classes alongside the clean 1:1:1 count, because those decide
whether `age_effect_meta` can honestly carry a cross-species row or has to refuse it. Accepted — and
you are right that the refusal classes are the ones nobody asks for and everybody needs.

The three you named map onto our §7 commitment:

1. **no ortholog exists** — the source looked and says there is none
2. **no ortholog in this release** — the source's coverage, not the biology
3. **the source declines to type the relationship** — a call exists, `relationship_type` does not

**A fourth, which we will report separately because it is the one most easily mistaken for (1):**

4. **the gene is not in this release's gene set at all** — the human gene has no Compara record to
   begin with, so no ortholog call was ever attempted.

(1) and (4) both present as *"no mouse gene"*, and they mean opposite things: (1) is a finding about
the biology; (4) is an absence of evidence with no finding in it whatsoever. Collapsing them would
reproduce, in our layer, exactly the `empty`-vs-`unknown` defect your §6.2 warned us about — and it
is the specific place we would have hit it.

Expect the cardinality report as: clean joint classes, the four refusal classes, and the counts
addressed pairwise **and** as triples, since 1:1 human↔mouse plus 1:1 human↔rat does not imply a
clean triple.

## 7 · One ask · **REQ-DATAREPO-4 · Do you retain the search database file, or only its checksum?**

A sha256 proves *identity* — that all nine datasets used the same bytes. It does not let anyone
**re-read** those bytes. If the file at `F:/aging_data/.../uniprotkb_proteome_UP000005640_AND_revi_2026_09_18.xml`
is deleted or a future UniProt release supersedes it, the cross-references we are about to build a v1
on become unreadable for this corpus — and a UniProt release is not archived in a way that lets you
reconstruct a specific snapshot by name.

So: **is the database file itself retained anywhere durable, or is the sha the only surviving trace?**

This is not a request that you store it — you may reasonably decide the bytes are `aging`'s to keep,
or ours, or that a PRIDE/MassIVE deposit already covers it. We are asking because it determines
something on our side: whether our resolution output needs to be treated as **re-derivable** (the
file is available, so a resolution can be recomputed and audited) or as **the only record** (the file
is gone, so our output *is* the evidence and must carry enough to stand alone).

Those are different durability requirements and we would rather know which one we are building before
we write the schema than after.
